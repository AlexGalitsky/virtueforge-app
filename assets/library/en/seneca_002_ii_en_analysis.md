This poetic rendition is an interpretation of Seneca's Second Letter from his Moral Epistles, traditionally titled "On Travel and Reading" (De discursu et lectione).

## Essence of the Work
In this letter, Seneca advises Lucilius on the importance of finding an inner anchor and not dissipating one's energies. The poet captures the key Stoic ideas:

* The harm of constant wandering. Frequent moves and vagrancy are signs of a diseased, disordered soul. To find peace, one must learn to be alone with oneself, not flee from oneself.
* The quality of reading surpasses quantity. Seneca criticizes the habit of reading everything indiscriminately (akin to modern "scrolling" or chasing after book releases). One should read few but profound authors (classics) so that their thoughts "digest," like healthy food.
* The metaphor of constant change. Frequent changes in books, places, and remedies only harm. A plant that is constantly transplanted never roots and strengthens.
* True wealth according to Epicurus. Seneca, though a Stoic, cites Epicurus, reminding us that the wealthy are not those with many possessions, but those who can limit their desires. Poverty coupled with joy and peace is true wealth.

## Features of This Version
The poet skillfully modernizes ancient imagery while preserving the meter (five-foot iambic) and structure of the original:

* Instead of the classical "many dishes only burden the stomach," the poet uses the ironic phrase "not sweet figs, but oxygen."
* References to "a tight purse" and "ultra-new style" make the text accessible to a modern reader while retaining a light archaic flavor.
* The poem traditionally ends with the classical Roman farewell "Be well" (Vale), with which Seneca concluded each of his letters.
