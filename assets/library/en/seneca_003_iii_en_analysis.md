## Essence of the Work
In this epistle, Seneca addresses a psychological error of Lucilius, who has labeled a person as a "friend" yet hesitates to confide a secret to him. The philosopher outlines the formula for genuine relationships:

* Division of judgment and trust. In friendship, there are two stages. Initially, one must carefully and rigorously choose a person ("First, judge: is he worthy of friendship?"). Once the choice is made, one must open up entirely and trust as one would trust oneself.
* Suspicion breeds betrayal. Seneca astutely observes that if one constantly expects betrayal from a friend, one teaches them to deceive and betray.
* Two extremes of trust. Equally mistaken are those who confide their soul to the first person they meet and those who, out of fear, isolate themselves ("He who trusts all is nobler than all...").
* Seeking the golden mean. At the end of the letter, Seneca transitions to balance in life through a quote from the Roman tragedian Pomponius. The philosopher condemns both perpetual busyness and complete seclusion in "darkness." True wisdom lies in alternating between tranquility and action, following the laws of nature.

## Features of This Version
The translator maintains fidelity to the iambic rhythmic pattern of the first two letters and accurately conveys the Stoic antitheses:

* The aphoristic line "Decide — is he your friend or foe?" sharply captures the essence of Seneca's main reproach to Lucilius.
* The phrase "chance master" beautifully replaces the Latin term for an ordinary person or a casual acquaintance, mistakenly given the status of a close friend.
* The poem organically balances between lofty philosophy and conversational poetry, concluding with the unchanged formula of farewell.
