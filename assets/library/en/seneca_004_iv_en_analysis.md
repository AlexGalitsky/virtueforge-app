This poetic rendition is an interpretation of Seneca's Fourth Letter to Lucilius, which addresses the human fear of death (De morte contemnenda).

## Essence of the Work
In this letter, Seneca explains that true freedom and peace of mind are unattainable while one fears death. The poet captures the key Stoic paradoxes with precision:

* The childishness in the face of death. People fear death as children fear the dark ("Childishness is the vice of the aged"). This fear is illusory, for death is a natural "tax" that all must pay.
* The principle of immediacy. Death cannot be feared because it does not coexist with life: while we live, it is not present, and when it arrives, we are no longer there. It instantly "becomes yesterday's day."
* The disdain for life from fear. Seneca notes the paradox that some people, exhausted by the fear of death, take their own lives out of fear. True courage lies in viewing life calmly and dispassionately.
* The illusion of safety under power. Seneca cites historical examples (Pompey, Lepidus, Caligula, murdered by Hiera), showing that rulers and tyrants die as easily as common people. Moreover, anyone can take a life—be it a slave or an accident.
* The ultimate sentence. A biting thought: it is pointless to fear a death sentence from an enemy or judge, for "from birth you are sentenced to death."
* Natural wealth. At the end of the letter, Seneca returns to the theme of basic needs. Nature requires little: not to starve, not to thirst, not to freeze. Everything beyond this is slavery to imagined goods.
