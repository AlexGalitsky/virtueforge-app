### Philosophical Context
Letter XXVII by Seneca is a response to Lucilius' request for advice on borrowed virtue. This text is part of a chain of philosophical judgments and thoughts that Seneca develops in his works.

### Essence
- Borrowed virtue is a form of virtue that can be "borrowed" or "acquired," rather than being inherent to a person.
- Seneca argues that borrowed virtue is more accessible and practical than natural virtue.
- He provides an example of Calvisius Sabinus, who wished to become a cultured person but failed due to his poor memory.
- Seneca also emphasizes the importance of anxiety and suffering in the development of virtue.

### Form of This Version
Letter XXVII by Seneca is written in prose.

### Connection to Virtue
Borrowed virtue is linked to the idea that virtue is not natural but can be acquired through anxiety and suffering. This aligns with the notion that virtue is a "cup not empty," in which one can find joy.

### Question to Ponder
How can we cultivate our virtue if it is not natural to us?
