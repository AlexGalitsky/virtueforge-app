# The Path to Salvation (Letter LII)

Dear Seneca,

The multitude is drawn to the allure of pleasure,
Why do you wear a face of stern wisdom?

You understand that this is simply foolishness,
But to turn away is little in us.

We prefer the life of poverty over the hardships of virtue.

It is fitting for all who have understood the will of God
And are following in its path.

Others follow by example, strictly adhering
To the first throughout their days.

There are those who are led astray by a false guide,
As was Epictetus' teacher, Hermeros.

The desire for salvation is great,
But merit is greater - those who have struggled to be saved.

Imagine two buildings constructed on different foundations:

One built on rock, and the other on sand...

Here lies the treasure of creation,
While there is heavy labor in applying one's hand.

One is built by nature, wise and unyielding,
The other is shaped by hard work.

Merit is greater than those who have struggled year after year,
Their flaws will eventually wear off.

"May eternal conflict bring us peace!
Let us only rest when we are dead!"

Call upon your ancestors for help,
As you see the image of good fortune and love in their lives.

Let the fools, known by all, waste their time talking idly at home...

Listen to those who act with integrity and honesty,
And those whose words cannot be deceived:

Do not listen to the boasts of orators!

There is one who speaks simply, without pretension,
But wants to make us a little better.

Should you praise a doctor with a knife?
Be silent! There was an agreement with Pythagoras:
Students must remain silent for five years.

Whoever believes in the empty words of flatterers
Shows their foolishness.

Praise is due to those who are wise, like Ovid,
Not because they want to entertain the crowd:

The enthusiasm of fools is not joy,
But rather annoyance when there is no reason to praise them...

Every thing has its own sign,
And in our characters, there are many flaws:

In the mad, there is a sense of reality,
In the immoral, there is a twisted hand.

When the multitude, uncontrolled by emotions,
Is willing to give everything to the philosopher...

He is not a philosopher, but a servant of wisdom:
Wisdom deserves praise.

Let young men, when their minds are stirred,
Express their fervent passion...

But let good deeds be more important than words:

There is a gap between the first and second.

The philosopher who presents himself as such,
Is like a courtesan or a bird with an open wing,
Not worthy of praise - mere foolishness.

Get out of the temple, traders!

Be healthy.
